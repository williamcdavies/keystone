Drift velocity is the average velocity attained by charged particles in a material due to an [[Electric fields|Electric field]]. In general, an electron in a conductor will propagate randomly at the [[Fermi velocity]], resulting in an average velocity of zero.

Drift velocity is proportional to [[Electric current]]. In a resistive material, it is also proportional to the magnitude of an external electric field. Thus [[Ohm's law]] can be explained in terms of drift velocity. The law's most elementary expressions is:
$$u = \mu E$$

where $u$ is the drift velocity, $\mu$ is the material's electron mobility, and $E$ is the electric field.

The formula for evaluating the drift velocity of charge carriers in a material of constant cross-sectional area is given by:
$$u = \frac{j}{nq}$$

where $u$ is the drift velocity of electrons, $j$ is the [[Electric current density]] flowing through the material, $n$ is the charge-carrier number density, and $q$ is the charge on the charge-carrier.

This can also be written as:
$$j = nqu$$

But the electric current density and drift velocity, $j$, and $u$, are in fact vectors, so this relationship is often written as:
$$\vec{J} = \rho \vec{u}$$

where
$$\rho = nq$$

is the charge density.

In terms of the basic properties of the right-cylindrical electric current-carrying metallic ohmic conductor, where the charge-carriers are electrons, this expression can be rewritten as:
$$u \frac{m \sigma \Delta V}{ \rho efl}$$

where $u$ is again the drift velocity of the electrons, $m$ is the molecular mass of the metal, $\sigma$ is the [[Electrical conductivity]] of the medium at the temperature considered, $\Delta V$ is the [[Electric potential]] applied across the conductor, $\rho$ is the [[Density]] of the conductor, $e$ is the elementary charge, $f$ is the number of free electrons per atom, and $l$ is the length of the conductor.
