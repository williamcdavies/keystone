Compound propositions are [[Propositions]] formed from existing propositions using [[Logical operators]]. 

A compound proposition is satisfiable if there is an assignment of truth values to its variables that makes it true. When no such assignments exist, the compound proposition is unsatisfiable. A particular assignment of truth values that makes a compound proposition true is called a solution. However, to show that a proposition is unsatisfiable, every assignment of truth values must be proven to be false.

## References
Discrete Mathematics and Its Applications Sec. 1.3.1 - Kenneth H. Rosen
> "A compound propositions that is always true, no matter what the truth values of the propositional variables that occur in it, is called a *tautology*. A compound propositions that is always false is called a *contradiction*. A compound propositions that is neither a tautology nor a contradiction is called *contingency*."

Discrete Mathematics and Its Applications Sec. 1.3.2 - Kenneth H. Rosen
> "The compound propositions $p$ and $q$ are called *logically equivalent* if $p \leftrightarrow q$ is a tautology. The notation $p \equiv q$ denotes that $p$ and $q$ are logically equivalent."
